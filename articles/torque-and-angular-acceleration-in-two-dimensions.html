Torque and Angular Acceleration in Two Dimensions

Article written by Trevor Kafka
October 2026

This article determines the conditions under which the formula $\boldsymbol \tau = I \boldsymbol \alpha$ holds for a rigid body moving in two dimensions, with torques taken about an arbitrary point $p$. Key topics include:

  • Baseline assumptions: Newton's laws, 2D rigid-body kinematics, and the definitions of torque, center of mass, and moment of inertia.
  • Derivation of the general relation $\boldsymbol \tau^\text{ext}_p = M \mathbf r_\text{cm}' \times \mathbf a_q + I_p \boldsymbol \alpha$.
  • Necessary and sufficient conditions for $\boldsymbol \tau^\text{ext}_p = I_p \boldsymbol \alpha$.
  • The locus of points satisfying these conditions: a circle through the center of mass.
  • Animated examples.

The formula holds about the center of mass, about any point where the coinciding point of the body has zero acceleration, and about any point where that acceleration points along the line to the center of mass. The derivation assumes familiarity with vectors, cross products, and introductory rigid-body mechanics.

Setup and Notation

$p, q$ $i,\ m_i$ $\mathrm{cm}$ $\mathbf r_i' = \mathbf r_i - \mathbf r_q$ $\mathbf r_\text{cm}' = \mathbf r_\text{cm} - \mathbf r_q$ $M,\ \boldsymbol \omega,\ \boldsymbol \alpha$

The diagram shows a freely moving rigid body of arbitrary shape with total mass $M$, angular velocity $\boldsymbol \omega$, and angular acceleration $\boldsymbol \alpha$. In it, $p$ is an arbitrary point about which torques are measured; it need not be fixed and may move in any way, even accelerating. Meanwhile, $q$ is the point of the body that coincides with $p$ at the instant considered. The two occupy the same location at that instant, but $q$ moves with the body while $p$ moves independently of it. The body is made up of particles labeled by $i$, each with mass $m_i$, and $\mathrm{cm}$ marks the body's center of mass (see definition ). All of these particles lie in the plane of motion, so every position vector below lies in that plane as well. With $\mathbf r_i$, $\mathbf r_q$, and $\mathbf r_\text{cm}$ denoting the positions of particle $i$, point $q$, and the center of mass, the vectors $\mathbf r_i' = \mathbf r_i - \mathbf r_q$ and $\mathbf r_\text{cm}' = \mathbf r_\text{cm} - \mathbf r_q$ give the positions of particle $i$ and of the center of mass relative to $q$. Since $q$ and $p$ coincide at the instant considered, these are also the positions relative to $p$, so they can be used to compute torques about $p$.

Baseline Assumptions

In order to derive the main results presented in this article, we must first establish a baseline set of axioms, kinematic results, and definitions. Here, $\text{int}$ and $\text{ext}$ serve to label internal and external forces and torques, respectively.

Axioms

  1. Newton's 2nd Law: $\mathbf F_i = \mathbf F^\text{int}_i + \mathbf F^\text{ext}_i = m_i \mathbf a_i$
  2. Newton's 3rd Law (Strong Form): $\forall \mathbf r : \sum_i (\mathbf r_i - \mathbf r) \times \mathbf F^\text{int}_i = \mathbf 0$

Rigid-Body Kinematics in 2D

  1. 2D Rigid-Body Acceleration Transformation: $\mathbf a_i = \mathbf a_q + \boldsymbol \alpha \times \mathbf r_i' - \omega^2 \mathbf r_i'$
  2. 2D Motion Identity: $\mathbf r_i' \times (\boldsymbol \alpha \times \mathbf r_i') = r_i'^2 \boldsymbol \alpha$

Definitions

  1. Torque: $\boldsymbol \tau^\text{ext}_{p,i} = \mathbf r_i' \times \mathbf F^\text{ext}_i$
  2. Center of Mass: $\mathbf r_\text{cm}' = \frac{1}{M} \sum_i m_i \mathbf r_i'$
  3. Moment of Inertia About $p$: $I_p = \sum_i m_i r_i'^2$
Show derivation of item 3

The angular velocity $\boldsymbol \omega$ of a rigid object is defined such that for any two points $i$ and $j$ on the object, we have $$\frac{d}{dt} (\mathbf r_i - \mathbf r_j) = \boldsymbol \omega \times (\mathbf r_i - \mathbf r_j).$$ Hence for points $i$ and $q$ we have the following. $$\mathbf v_i - \mathbf v_q = \boldsymbol \omega \times (\mathbf r_i - \mathbf r_q)$$ $$\mathbf v_i'= \boldsymbol \omega \times \mathbf r_i'$$ Here $\mathbf v_i' = \mathbf v_i - \mathbf v_q = \frac{d}{dt} \mathbf r_i'$ is the velocity of particle $i$ relative to $q$. Differentiate with respect to time, writing $\mathbf a_i' = \mathbf a_i - \mathbf a_q = \frac{d}{dt} \mathbf v_i'$. $$\mathbf a_i' = \boldsymbol \alpha \times \mathbf r_i' + \boldsymbol \omega \times \mathbf v_i'$$ $$\mathbf a_i' = \boldsymbol \alpha \times \mathbf r_i' + \boldsymbol \omega \times ( \boldsymbol \omega \times \mathbf r_i' )$$ Expand the resulting vector product using the identity $\mathbf A \times (\mathbf B \times \mathbf C) = \mathbf B (\mathbf A \cdot \mathbf C) - \mathbf C (\mathbf A \cdot \mathbf B)$. The first term of the expansion vanishes since $\boldsymbol \omega \perp \mathbf r_i'$ for 2D motion. $$\mathbf a_i' = \boldsymbol \alpha \times \mathbf r_i' + \underbrace{\boldsymbol \omega ( \boldsymbol \omega \cdot \mathbf r_i' )}_{\mathbf 0} - \omega^2 \mathbf r_i'$$ $$\mathbf a_i' = \boldsymbol \alpha \times \mathbf r_i' - \omega^2 \mathbf r_i'$$ Substituting $\mathbf a_i' = \mathbf a_i - \mathbf a_q$ gives item . $$\mathbf a_i = \mathbf a_q + \boldsymbol \alpha \times \mathbf r_i' - \omega^2 \mathbf r_i'$$

Show derivation of item 4

Expand the vector triple product using the identity $\mathbf A \times (\mathbf B \times \mathbf C) = \mathbf B (\mathbf A \cdot \mathbf C) - \mathbf C (\mathbf A \cdot \mathbf B)$. $$\mathbf r_i' \times (\boldsymbol \alpha \times \mathbf r_i') = \boldsymbol \alpha (\mathbf r_i' \cdot \mathbf r_i') - \mathbf r_i' (\underbrace{\mathbf r_i' \cdot \boldsymbol \alpha}_{0}) = r_i'^2 \boldsymbol \alpha$$ The last term vanishes since $\mathbf r_i' \perp \boldsymbol \alpha$ for 2D motion.

Derivation of the Relationship Between Torque and Angular Acceleration

Begin with the definition of torque to compute the net external torque $\boldsymbol \tau^\text{ext}_p$ about point $p$.

$$\boldsymbol \tau^\text{ext}_p = \sum_i \boldsymbol \tau^\text{ext}_{p,i} = \sum_i \mathbf r_i' \times \mathbf F^\text{ext}_i$$

Substitute in Newton's 2nd Law . The second term vanishes by the strong form of Newton's 3rd Law .

$$\boldsymbol \tau^\text{ext}_p = \sum_i \mathbf r_i' \times m_i \mathbf a_i - \underbrace{\sum_i \mathbf r_i' \times \mathbf F^\text{int}_i}_{\mathbf 0}$$

Substitute the 2D rigid-body acceleration relation , resulting in three terms: a translational term, a tangential term, and a centripetal term.

  • The translational term can be rewritten in terms of the center of mass by means of its definition .
  • The tangential term simplifies by the 2D motion identity and can then be further simplified by means of the definition of the moment of inertia .
  • The centripetal term vanishes as a direct consequence of the nature of its cross product.
$$\boldsymbol \tau^\text{ext}_p = \overbrace{\rule{0pt}{1.6em}\underbrace{ \sum_i m_i \mathbf r_i' }_{M \mathbf r_\text{cm}'} \times \mathbf a_q}^\text{translational term} + \overbrace{\rule{0pt}{1.6em}\underbrace{\sum_i m_i \underbrace{\rule[-1em]{0pt}{1em} \mathbf r_i' \times (\boldsymbol \alpha \times \mathbf r_i')}_{r_i'^2 \boldsymbol \alpha}}_{I_p \boldsymbol \alpha}}^\text{tangential term} - \overbrace{\rule{0pt}{1.6em}\omega^2 \sum_i m_i \underbrace{\rule[-1em]{0pt}{1em} \mathbf r_i' \times \mathbf r_i'}_{\mathbf 0}}^\text{centripetal term}$$ $$\boxed{\rule[-0.7em]{0pt}{2.2em} \quad \boldsymbol \tau^\text{ext}_p = M \mathbf r_\text{cm}' \times \mathbf a_q + I_p \boldsymbol \alpha \quad}$$

When Does the High-School Formula $\boldsymbol \tau^\text{ext}_p = I_p \boldsymbol \alpha$ Hold?

Based on the final result of the previous section, the high-school formula $\boldsymbol \tau^\text{ext}_p = I_p \boldsymbol \alpha$ holds only when $\mathbf r_\text{cm}' \times \mathbf a_q$ vanishes, which occurs when $\mathbf r_\text{cm}' = \mathbf 0$, $\mathbf a_q = \mathbf 0$, or $\mathbf r_\text{cm}' \parallel \mathbf a_q$. In words, these three conditions are as follows.

Theorem. For a rigid object moving in two dimensions, $\boldsymbol \tau^\text{ext}_p = I_p \boldsymbol \alpha$ if and only if any one of the following conditions is satisfied.

  1. Point $p$ is the center of mass of the object.
  2. The velocity of the point of the body that coincides with point $p$ is instantaneously constant.
  3. The nonzero acceleration of the point of the body that coincides with point $p$ points either directly toward or away from the center of mass of the object.

We can nail down further detail: $\mathbf a_q$ can be related to the acceleration of the center of mass by the 2D rigid-body acceleration relation stated earlier , using the center of mass as point $i$.

$$\mathbf a_\text{cm} = \mathbf a_q + \boldsymbol \alpha \times \mathbf r_\text{cm}' - \omega^2 \mathbf r_\text{cm}' \label{eq:a-cm}$$

Substituting this result into $\mathbf r_\text{cm}' \times \mathbf a_q = \mathbf 0$ once again gives a translational term, a tangential term, and a centripetal term, each of which simplifies in a manner similar to what we saw in the previous section.

$$\overbrace{\rule{0pt}{1.6em} \mathbf r_\text{cm}' \times \mathbf a_\text{cm}}^{\mathclap{\text{translational term}}} - \overbrace{\rule{0pt}{1.6em} \underbrace{\rule[-1em]{0pt}{1em} \mathbf r_\text{cm}' \times (\boldsymbol \alpha \times \mathbf r_\text{cm}')}_{r_\text{cm}'^2 \boldsymbol \alpha}}^\text{tangential term} + \overbrace{\rule{0pt}{1.6em} \omega^2 \underbrace{\rule[-1em]{0pt}{1em} \mathbf r_\text{cm}' \times \mathbf r_\text{cm}'}_{\mathbf 0}}^\text{centripetal term} = \mathbf 0$$ $$\mathbf r_\text{cm}' \times \mathbf a_\text{cm} = r_\text{cm}'^2 \boldsymbol \alpha \label{eq:cm-condition}$$

Taking magnitudes, with $\theta$ the angle between $\mathbf r_\text{cm}'$ and $\mathbf a_\text{cm}$, gives the following. If either vector is $\mathbf 0$, the angle $\theta$ is undefined, but both sides then vanish, so the equation holds for any choice of $\theta$.

$$ r_\text{cm}' a_\text{cm} \sin \theta = r_\text{cm}'^2 \alpha$$

This gives the polar equation of a circle of diameter $d = \frac{a_\text{cm}}{\alpha}$.

$$r_\text{cm}' = d \sin \theta$$

The locus of all points described by , , and is hence this circle. The accompanying figure shows a possible circle for our example body: the acceleration $\mathbf a_q$ of the point of the body coinciding with $p$, which lies along the line toward the center of mass; the center of mass with its acceleration $\mathbf a_\text{cm}$ tangent to the circle; the vector $\mathbf d$ running from the center of mass across the diameter of the circle to the point $\xi$; and the resulting right triangle with angle $\theta$ and side lengths $r_\text{cm}'$ and $d$.

$p, q$ $\xi$ $\mathrm{cm}$ $\mathbf a_\text{cm}$ $\mathbf d$ $\mathbf a_q$ $r_\text{cm}'$ $\theta$ $\theta$ $\boldsymbol \alpha$

The points corresponding to $\theta = 0$ and $\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}$ are the center of mass and point $\xi$, respectively. When $p$ is at $\xi$, we have $r_\text{cm}' = d$, giving $r_\text{cm}' \alpha = a_\text{cm}$. Accounting for the mutual orthogonality, relative magnitudes, and relative orientations of the three vectors $\mathbf a_\text{cm}$, $\boldsymbol \alpha$, and $\mathbf r_\text{cm}'$, we can compare with \eqref{eq:cm-condition} to obtain the following.

$$\mathbf a_\text{cm} = \boldsymbol \alpha \times \mathbf r_\text{cm}' \label{eq:a-cm-xi}$$

For $p$ at $\xi$, we have $\mathbf r_\text{cm}' = -\mathbf d$. Subtracting \eqref{eq:a-cm-xi} from equation \eqref{eq:a-cm} leaves $\mathbf a_q - \omega^2 \mathbf r_\text{cm}' = \mathbf 0$, so $\mathbf a_q = \omega^2 \mathbf r_\text{cm}' = -\omega^2 \mathbf d$, which points from $\xi$ directly toward the center of mass, consistent with .

The vector $\mathbf d$ can also be expressed directly in terms of the motion of the body. Substituting $\mathbf r_\text{cm}' = -\mathbf d$ into the expression for $\mathbf a_\text{cm}$ above gives the following.

$$\boxed{\rule[-0.7em]{0pt}{2.2em} \quad \mathbf a_\text{cm} = \mathbf d \times \boldsymbol \alpha \quad}$$

Since $\mathbf d$ lies in the plane of motion, this equation uniquely defines $\mathbf d$ and hence the entire circle itself (given $\boldsymbol \alpha \neq \mathbf 0$).
