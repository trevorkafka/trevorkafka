Torque and Angular Acceleration in Two Dimensions

Article written by Trevor Kafka
October 2026

The diagram below shows a freely moving rigid body of arbitrary shape with total mass $M$, angular velocity $\boldsymbol \omega$, and angular acceleration $\boldsymbol \alpha$. In it, $p$ is an arbitrary point about which torques are measured; it need not be fixed and may move in any way, even accelerating. Meanwhile, $q$ is the point of the body that coincides with $p$ at the instant considered. The two occupy the same location at that instant, but $q$ moves with the body while $p$ moves independently of it. The body is made up of particles labeled by $i$, each with mass $m_i$, and $\mathrm{cm}$ marks the body's center of mass (see definition ). With $\mathbf r_i$, $\mathbf r_q$, and $\mathbf r_\text{cm}$ denoting the positions of particle $i$, point $q$, and the center of mass, the vectors $\mathbf r_i' = \mathbf r_i - \mathbf r_q$ and $\mathbf r_\text{cm}' = \mathbf r_\text{cm} - \mathbf r_q$ give the positions of particle $i$ and of the center of mass relative to $q$. Since $q$ and $p$ coincide at the instant considered, these are also the positions relative to $p$, so they can be used to compute torques about $p$.

Baseline Assumptions

In order to derive the main results presented in this article, we must first establish a baseline set of axioms, kinematic results, and definitions. Here, $\text{int}$ and $\text{ext}$ serve to label internal and external forces and torques, respectively.

Axioms

  1. Newton's 2nd Law: $\mathbf F_i = \mathbf F^\text{int}_i + \mathbf F^\text{ext}_i = m_i \mathbf a_i$
  2. Newton's 3rd Law (Strong Form): $\forall \mathbf r : \sum_i (\mathbf r_i - \mathbf r) \times \mathbf F^\text{int}_i = 0$

Rigid-Body Kinematics in 2D

  1. 2D Rigid-Body Acceleration: $\mathbf a_i = \mathbf a_q + \boldsymbol \alpha \times \mathbf r_i' - \omega^2 \mathbf r_i'$
  2. 2D Motion Identity: $\mathbf r_i' \times (\boldsymbol \alpha \times \mathbf r_i') = {r_i'}^2 \boldsymbol \alpha$

Definitions

  1. Torque: $\boldsymbol \tau^\text{ext}_{p,i} = \mathbf r_i' \times \mathbf F^\text{ext}_i$
  2. Center of Mass: $\sum_i m_i \mathbf r_i' = M \mathbf r_\text{cm}'$
  3. Moment of Inertia About $p$: $I_p = \sum_i m_i {r_i'}^2$
Show derivation of item 3

The angular velocity $\boldsymbol \omega$ of a rigid object is defined such that for any two points $i$ and $j$ on the object, we have $$\frac{d}{dt} (\mathbf r_i - \mathbf r_j) = \boldsymbol \omega \times (\mathbf r_i - \mathbf r_j).$$ Hence for points $i$ and $q$ we have the following. $$\mathbf v_i - \mathbf v_q = \boldsymbol \omega \times (\mathbf r_i - \mathbf r_q)$$ $$\mathbf v_i'= \boldsymbol \omega \times \mathbf r_i'$$ Here $\mathbf v_i' = \mathbf v_i - \mathbf v_q = \frac{d}{dt} \mathbf r_i'$ is the velocity of particle $i$ relative to $q$. Differentiate with respect to time, writing $\mathbf a_i' = \mathbf a_i - \mathbf a_q = \frac{d}{dt} \mathbf v_i'$. $$\mathbf a_i' = \boldsymbol \alpha \times \mathbf r_i' + \boldsymbol \omega \times \mathbf v_i'$$ $$\mathbf a_i' = \boldsymbol \alpha \times \mathbf r_i' + \boldsymbol \omega \times ( \boldsymbol \omega \times \mathbf r_i' )$$ Expand the resulting vector product using the identity $\mathbf A \times (\mathbf B \times \mathbf C) = \mathbf B (\mathbf A \cdot \mathbf C) - \mathbf C (\mathbf A \cdot \mathbf B)$. The first term of the expansion vanishes since $\boldsymbol \omega \perp \mathbf r_i'$ for 2D motion. $$\mathbf a_i' = \boldsymbol \alpha \times \mathbf r_i' + \underbrace{\boldsymbol \omega ( \boldsymbol \omega \cdot \mathbf r_i' )}_0 - \omega^2 \mathbf r_i'$$ $$\mathbf a_i' = \boldsymbol \alpha \times \mathbf r_i' - \omega^2 \mathbf r_i'$$ Substituting $\mathbf a_i' = \mathbf a_i - \mathbf a_q$ gives item . $$\mathbf a_i = \mathbf a_q + \boldsymbol \alpha \times \mathbf r_i' - \omega^2 \mathbf r_i'$$

Show derivation of item 4

Expand the vector triple product using the identity $\mathbf A \times (\mathbf B \times \mathbf C) = \mathbf B (\mathbf A \cdot \mathbf C) - \mathbf C (\mathbf A \cdot \mathbf B)$. $$\mathbf r_i' \times (\boldsymbol \alpha \times \mathbf r_i') = \boldsymbol \alpha (\mathbf r_i' \cdot \mathbf r_i') - \mathbf r_i' (\underbrace{\mathbf r_i' \cdot \boldsymbol \alpha}_{0}) = {r_i'}^2 \boldsymbol \alpha$$ The last term vanishes since $\mathbf r_i' \perp \boldsymbol \alpha$ for 2D motion.

Derivation of the Relationship Between Torque and Angular Acceleration

First, begin with the definition of torque () to compute the net external torque $\boldsymbol \tau^\text{ext}_p$ about point $p$.

$$\boldsymbol \tau^\text{ext}_p = \sum_i \boldsymbol \tau^\text{ext}_{p,i} = \sum_i \mathbf r_i' \times \mathbf F^\text{ext}_i$$

Substitute in Newton's 2nd Law (). The second term vanishes by the strong form of Newton's 3rd Law ().

$$\boldsymbol \tau^\text{ext}_p = \sum_i \mathbf r_i' \times m_i \mathbf a_i - \underbrace{\sum_i \mathbf r_i' \times \mathbf F^\text{int}_i}_{0}$$

Substitute the 2D rigid-body acceleration (), resulting in three terms: a translational term, a tangential term, and a centripetal term.

  • The translational term can be rewritten in terms of the center of mass by means of its definition ().
  • The tangential term simplifies by the 2D motion identity (), which can furthermore be simplified by means of the definition of the moment of inertia ().
  • The centripetal term vanishes as a direct consequence of the nature of its cross product.
$$\boldsymbol \tau^\text{ext}_p = \overbrace{\underbrace{ \sum_i m_i \mathbf r_i' }_{M \mathbf r_\text{cm}'} \times \mathbf a_q}^\text{translational term} + \overbrace{\underbrace{\sum_i m_i \underbrace{\mathbf r_i' \times (\boldsymbol \alpha \times \mathbf r_i')}_{{r_i'}^2 \boldsymbol \alpha}}_{I_p \boldsymbol \alpha}}^\text{tangential term} - \overbrace{\omega^2 \sum_i m_i \underbrace{\mathbf r_i' \times \mathbf r_i'}_0}^\text{centripetal term}$$ $$\boxed{\boldsymbol \tau^\text{ext}_p = M \mathbf r_\text{cm}' \times \mathbf a_q + I_p \boldsymbol \alpha}$$

When Does the High School Formula $\boldsymbol \tau^\text{ext}_p = I_p \boldsymbol \alpha$ Hold?

Based on the final result of the previous section, the high school formula $\boldsymbol \tau^\text{ext}_p = I_p \boldsymbol \alpha$ holds only when $M \mathbf r_\text{cm}' \times \mathbf a_q$ vanishes, which occurs when $\mathbf r_\text{cm}' = 0$, $\mathbf a_q=0$, or $\mathbf r_\text{cm}' \parallel \mathbf a_q$. In words, these three conditions are as follows.

Theorem. For a rigid object moving in two dimensions, $\boldsymbol \tau^\text{ext}_p = I_p \boldsymbol \alpha$ if and only if any one of the following conditions is satisfied.

  1. Point $p$ is the center of mass of the object.
  2. The velocity of the point of the body that coincides with point $p$ is instantaneously constant.
  3. The nonzero acceleration of the point of the body that coincides with point $p$ points either directly towards or away from the center of mass of the object.
